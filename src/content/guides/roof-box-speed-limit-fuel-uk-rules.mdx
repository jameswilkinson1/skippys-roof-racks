UK law doesn't mention roof boxes by name, which is why the rules confuse so many people. What it does cover is speed limits, secure loads and not overloading your car. Thule's own safety instructions fill in the rest, and they're worth reading before a long trip.

## Is there a speed limit with a roof box?

Not a separate one. The normal UK limits apply, so a car with a roof box can still legally do 70 mph on a motorway or dual carriageway.

Thule's roof box safety instructions set an upper limit: your speed "must under no circumstances exceed 130 km/h" (about 80 mph). That's above the UK motorway limit, so in practice the legal limit is your ceiling here. Thule also says speed must always be adjusted to the load you're carrying and the conditions, including road type, road surface, wind and traffic.

That second part matters more than the headline number. Thule warns that a roof box changes how the car behaves, especially when loaded:

- it's more sensitive to side winds, so exposed roads and bridges feel different
- cornering changes, because the weight is higher up
- braking behaviour changes, so leave more room to stop

If you're carrying other kit on the roof alongside the box, check each product's manual, as every carrier has its own instructions.

After the first few miles of a trip, pull over somewhere safe and check the box is still firmly clamped to the bars and locked. Thule's instructions say to check this before and during every journey, and it takes a minute.

## Does a roof box use more fuel?

Yes, and mostly because of air resistance, which rises sharply as you go faster. Exact figures depend on the car, the box and your speed, but published advice from government sources gives a good idea:

| Source | What it covers | Effect on fuel |
|---|---|---|
| UK Department for Transport (2011 holiday driving advice) | Roof box at motorway speeds | Around 25% more fuel used |
| US Department of Energy, fueleconomy.gov (Oak Ridge National Laboratory research) | Large, blunt roof box, city driving | 2% to 8% worse economy |
| US Department of Energy, fueleconomy.gov | Large, blunt roof box, highway driving | 6% to 17% worse economy |
| US Department of Energy, fueleconomy.gov | Large, blunt roof box, 65 to 75 mph | 10% to 25% worse economy |
| US Department of Energy, fueleconomy.gov | Rear-mounted cargo box, highway driving | 1% to 5% worse economy |

The pattern is clear: around town the difference is small, while at a steady 70 mph it can be up to a quarter more fuel. Electric cars are affected too. Our guide to [roof boxes for electric cars](/guides/roof-boxes-for-electric-cars/) covers range in detail.

Ways to keep the cost down:

- **Ease off a little on the motorway.** Speed has the biggest effect.
- **Fit the box correctly.** Thule says the box must face forwards and sit parallel to the road, not at an angle, to avoid excess lifting forces.
- **Pick a sensible size and shape.** Thule describes the Motion 3 as having a wing-shaped profile with a forward-leaning nose for reduced drag. Bigger than you need just means more box in the wind.
- **Take it off when you don't need it.** Both Thule and the Department for Transport recommend removing the box when it isn't in use.

## The law on secure loads

Rule 98 of the Highway Code says you must not overload your vehicle, and that you must secure your load so it doesn't stick out dangerously.

The offence behind it is using a vehicle in a dangerous condition. Section 40A of the Road Traffic Act 1988 includes the weight, position or distribution of a load, and the way it's secured. According to the Highway Code's penalty table, that can mean a fine of up to £2,500 for a car and 3 penalty points.

For a roof box, staying on the right side of this comes down to three things: a box properly clamped to properly fitted bars, a lid that's closed and locked, and contents that can't shift around. Thule also says nothing should be attached to the outside of the box. Our guide on [how to pack a roof box](/guides/how-to-pack-a-roof-box/) covers weight distribution.

## Roof load limits

Every car has a maximum roof load, set by the manufacturer and printed in the handbook. Thule's safety instructions show how to work out what you're actually putting up there:

**Weight of roof rack + weight of roof box + weight of the load = your roof load**

That total must not exceed the car's limit. The box and bars have their own limits too: current Thule boxes such as the Motion 3 L and Force 3 L are rated to 75 kg. The lowest figure in the chain is the one you work to. And don't forget the car's overall maximum weight still applies once it's full of people and luggage.

## Height: car parks, garages and tunnels

This one catches people out. Thule warns that a roof rack and box can add up to 700 mm (about 27.5 in) to your car's height, depending on the rack. Thule specifically mentions low garage entrances, low clearances generally and overhanging branches.

A few habits help:

- measure the car's total height with the box fitted, and stick a note of it on the dashboard
- check height signs on multi-storey car parks, drive-throughs and barriers before you commit
- don't forget your own garage or carport when you get home

Crossing the Channel? LeShuttle says any vehicle over 1.85 m (about 6 ft 1 in), or with anything on the roof, goes in the single-deck carriages, so book the right category.

## Do I need to tell my insurer about a roof box?

It's sensible to. Insurers don't all treat roof boxes the same way. Some list roof bars and roof boxes as accessories that don't affect your premium, while others say a roof box can be classed as a modification. Failing to declare a modification can put a claim at risk.

A quick call or email to your insurer settles it. While you're there, ask whether the contents of the box are covered if it's broken into, as that varies between policies. The same applies to a hired box, since it's your car and your policy.

## Quick pre-trip checklist

- Box clamped to the bars, lid closed and locked, key removed (as Thule's instructions say)
- Total roof load within the handbook figure
- Car's new height noted
- Insurer told
- A quick stop after the first few miles to check everything is still tight
